After a hard call or shift

How to decompress after a hard call or shift

Some moments at work stay with you. A patient, a caller, a student, a customer, a scene. The shift keeps going, or it ends and you're expected to walk into your house like nothing happened. Here's how to reset, even when you only have a couple of minutes.

Why it follows you home

In a hard moment at work, your body does its job. It stays sharp, focused and on alert. But that alert state doesn't clock out when you do. It can show up as replaying the call, snapping at the people you love, not being able to sleep, or feeling numb.

None of that means you're not cut out for the work. It means you're human, and your body hasn't gotten the message that it's over.

Step 1 · Between calls or in the car

A 2-minute reset

Breathe out longer than you breathe in

In for 4, out for 6 to 8. You can do it with a headset on or at a nurses' station and nobody can tell.

Box breathing

In for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4. A lot of people in high-stress jobs use it because it's steady and easy to count.

Squeeze and release

Grip the steering wheel or press your palms together hard for 10 seconds, then let go. Notice the difference.

Mark the end of the shift

Wash your hands, change your shoes, or sit in the car for one song before you go inside. Small rituals tell your body the shift is over.

Step 2 · When you have a few minutes

Get it out of your head

You don't have to talk about it to process it. Write a few lines, privately:

  • What's the part I keep seeing or hearing?
  • What did I do well, even if the outcome wasn't what I wanted?
  • What do I need tonight? Sleep, food, company, quiet?

Putting it into words can turn it into something you carried, instead of something you're still carrying.

Step 3 · Before the day is over

Don't carry it alone

The people who get it most are often the ones you work with. A short message is enough:

  • "That call earlier is still with me. You good?"
  • "Rough one today. Want to grab food after shift?"
  • "I don't need to talk about it. I just don't want to be alone right now."

If your workplace has peer support or an employee assistance program, it's there for exactly this. Using it doesn't mean something is wrong with you.

Common questions

How do I stop thinking about a bad call?

You may not be able to stop it right away, and that's normal. Calming your body first helps, then writing down what keeps coming back. If it's still showing up after a few weeks, talk to a peer support person or a counselor.

How do I decompress after a shift before going home?

Give yourself a short transition: a few long breaths in the car, one song, a change of clothes or a walk around the block. It helps your body switch from work mode to home mode.

Is it normal to feel numb after a traumatic call?

Yes. Numbness is one of the ways people get through hard moments. It usually lifts with rest and time. If it doesn't, or it's affecting your sleep and relationships, that's a good reason to reach out for support.

If a call keeps coming back for weeks, with nightmares, flashbacks, or avoiding things that remind you of it, that's worth talking to a professional about, ideally someone who works with people in your field. If you're thinking about hurting yourself, call or text 988 any time. Veterans can press 1.

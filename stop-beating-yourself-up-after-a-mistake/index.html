After a mistake

How to stop beating yourself up after a mistake

You messed up. Maybe at work, maybe at home, maybe in front of people. Now your stomach is in a knot and your brain won't stop replaying it. Here's how to get out of the spiral and back to thinking clearly.

Why your brain won't let it go

Your brain treats a mistake like a threat. It replays it again and again, trying to make sure it never happens twice. In small doses, that's useful. Hours later, it just keeps you feeling awful and makes it harder to fix anything.

You can't think your way out of that loop while your body is still panicking. So calm down first.

Step 1 · About 2 minutes

Calm the panic

Breathe out longer than you breathe in

In for 4, out for 6 to 8. Or take two short breaths in through your nose and one long breath out. Repeat for a minute or two.

Name it plainly

Say it in one sentence: "I made a mistake and I feel embarrassed." Or scared, or guilty. Putting a plain name on the feeling takes some of its power away.

Feet on the floor

Press your feet into the ground and name five things you can see. It pulls your attention back into the room and out of the replay.

Step 2 · Before you decide anything

Separate the facts from the story

Write it out privately, in two short lists:

  • What actually happened. Just the facts, like a report.
  • What I'm telling myself about it. "I'm an idiot." "Everyone thinks I'm useless."

The second list is almost always harsher than the first. Then ask yourself: what would I say to a friend who made this exact mistake? Say that to yourself.

Step 3 · When you're ready

Decide what, if anything, to do

  • Can it be fixed? If so, what's the smallest next step?
  • Does someone need to know? Telling them early is almost always better than hiding it.
  • What would I do differently next time? One thing is enough.

If you need to tell someone, short and direct works best:

  • "I made a mistake on this. Here's what happened and here's what I'm doing to fix it."
  • "I got that wrong, and I'm sorry. I'll make sure it doesn't happen again."
  • "Can I get your help with something I messed up?"

And if there's nothing left to fix, the next step is letting it be over. Everyone you admire has done something like this.

Common questions

How do I stop replaying a mistake in my head?

Calm your body first, because the replay is fueled by stress. Then write down the facts and one thing you'd do differently. Once your brain sees there's a plan, it usually eases off.

Should I tell my boss I made a mistake?

Usually, yes, and sooner is better. Bring the facts and what you're doing to fix it. Most managers care far more about how you handle a mistake than about the mistake itself.

Is it normal to feel this bad over a small mistake?

Yes, especially when you're tired or stressed, or when you care a lot about doing well. The feeling is often bigger than the mistake. It fades faster when you stop arguing with it.

If you're feeling hopeless, or like people would be better off without you, please don't sit with that alone. Call or text 988 any time, day or night. In an emergency, call 911.
